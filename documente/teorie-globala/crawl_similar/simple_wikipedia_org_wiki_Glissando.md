In music a**glissando** is a slide up or down the notes of a[scale](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_\(music\) "Scale \(music\)"). The plural is “glissandi”. In[written music](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheet_music "Sheet music")the instruction to the player is often shortened to “gliss”. The word comes from the French “glisser” = “to slide”.
With the[singing](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singing "Singing")voice, or with an instrument such as the[trombone](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trombone "Trombone")or a[string instrument](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_instrument "String instrument")a glissando is a smooth slide in which the[pitch](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_\(music\) "Pitch \(music\)")gradually changes, becoming higher and higher. The trombonist can make a glissando by blowing whilst pushing out or pulling in the slide. The violinist can make a glissando by sliding a finger up or down the fingerboard.
With instruments such as the[piano](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano "Piano"),[xylophone](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylophone "Xylophone")or[harp](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harp "Harp")a glissando is a quick slide in which we hear one note after another because the pitches between the notes cannot be played. On the piano there are two ways to play a glissando: either on the white notes or on the black notes. At the end of the piano piece called _Ondine_ from _Gaspard de la Nuit_ by[Maurice Ravel](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Ravel "Maurice Ravel")both these types of glissando can be heard. The pianist has to play a glissando with tips of the fingers or the finger nails. It can be quite painful to practise them a lot. Harpists are very fond of playing glissandi. By changing the position of the harp pedals a harpist can change the scale which will sound when the glissando is played.
